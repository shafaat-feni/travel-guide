Bangladesh is known for its hospitality, lively public life, and strong family and community ties. Customs vary by region, generation, setting, and individual, so treat the guidance below as a helpful starting point rather than a set of rules that everyone follows. A little awareness, patience, and courtesy will usually help you navigate social situations comfortably.

## Dress Guidelines for Visitors

Bangladesh is generally socially conservative, particularly in rural communities and around religious sites. In Dhaka and other large cities, clothing is more varied, but modest attire is a practical choice in most public settings.

### General guidelines

- **For men:** Lightweight trousers or jeans with a shirt or polo shirt are suitable for most situations. Shorts are fine in some casual settings, but long trousers are a safer choice for religious sites, formal offices, and visits to local homes.
- **For women:** Loose-fitting clothes that cover the shoulders and at least the knees are a useful default. A lightweight scarf, locally called an *orna* or *orna/dupatta* depending on context, can be handy for covering the shoulders or head when appropriate.
- **For everyone:** Choose breathable fabrics for hot, humid weather. Avoid assuming that clothing accepted at a hotel or resort will be equally suitable at a village home or place of worship.

There is no single dress code for the whole country. Follow posted rules and observe what people in the setting are doing, while remembering that local customs differ.

## Visiting Religious Sites

Bangladesh has mosques, Hindu temples, Buddhist monasteries and other sacred places, as well as churches and shrines. Visitors may be welcome at some sites, while others restrict access during worship or are not open to tourists. Ask before entering if you are unsure.

### Mosques, shrines, temples and monasteries

- **Footwear:** Remove your shoes wherever requested, especially before entering mosque prayer halls, temples, and other sacred interiors. Look for signs or follow the guidance of site staff.
- **Clothing:** Cover your shoulders and knees as a respectful default. Some mosques require women to cover their hair; carry a scarf and follow the site's specific instructions. Requirements vary by location.
- **Behaviour:** Speak quietly, silence your phone, and do not walk in front of people who are praying if you can avoid it. Never interrupt a ceremony to take a photograph.
- **Photography:** Ask permission before photographing worshippers, religious ceremonies, or interiors. Some areas prohibit photography entirely; obey signs and staff instructions.
- **Access:** Do not enter restricted prayer areas or sacred spaces simply because a door is open. If a service is underway, wait for guidance or visit another time.

## Greetings and Everyday Interaction

- **Greetings:** “Assalamu alaikum” is a common Muslim greeting and is widely understood. “Hello” and “Good morning” are also appropriate. You can reply “Wa alaikum assalam” when someone greets you this way.
- **Handshakes:** Practices differ, especially between people of different genders. If you are unsure, offer a smile and a small nod rather than initiating physical contact. Let the other person indicate whether they are comfortable shaking hands.
- **Personal space:** Be patient in busy streets, markets, and transport hubs, where crowds can make personal space difficult to maintain. Avoid interpreting crowding automatically as rudeness.
- **Use your right hand when practical:** The right hand is traditionally preferred when giving or receiving food, money, or objects. This is a courtesy, not a reason to panic if you use your left hand accidentally.
- **Titles and respect:** A polite tone and respectful forms of address are appreciated. If you are unsure how to address someone, a simple greeting is fine.

Bangladesh is linguistically diverse. Bangla (Bengali) is the national language, and English is spoken by some people in tourism, business, and urban settings, but not everyone will understand it. Learning a few Bangla phrases can make everyday interactions friendlier. See the [main trip-planning guide](/plan-your-trip/) for more practical preparation.

## Hospitality and Dining Customs

Being offered tea, snacks, or food is common in many social settings. Hospitality varies from household to household, so respond warmly without assuming that every invitation carries the same expectations.

- **If invited into a home:** Follow your host's lead about removing shoes; in many homes, shoes are left at the entrance.
- **When offered refreshments:** A sincere thank-you is appreciated. If you cannot accept something, decline gently and explain briefly.
- **Eating with your hands:** Some Bangladeshi meals are traditionally eaten by hand, usually with the right hand. Cutlery may also be available, especially in restaurants. Follow your host's lead and use whichever option you are comfortable with.
- **Dietary needs:** If you have allergies or dietary restrictions, explain them clearly in advance where possible. Do not assume every dish is vegetarian or that ingredients are always obvious.
- **Paying the bill:** In restaurants, check the bill for any service charge before deciding whether to leave an additional tip. Tipping practices vary; a modest tip may be appreciated in some tourist-facing establishments but is not required in every situation.

## Photography and Asking Permission

People may be curious about foreign visitors, and you may be asked for a photo. You can politely decline. Apply the same courtesy when taking pictures of others.

- Ask permission before taking close-up portraits, particularly of children, workers, or people at religious sites.
- Do not photograph military, police, border, airport-security, or other restricted facilities where photography is prohibited.
- Be considerate in private homes, hospitals, schools, and places of worship.
- If someone appears uncomfortable, put your camera away without arguing.

## Public Behaviour, Affection and Alcohol

Public behaviour that is ordinary in one country may attract attention in another. In Bangladesh, discreet behaviour is generally the safer choice in unfamiliar settings.

- **Public affection:** Avoid conspicuous kissing or intimate physical contact in public, particularly in conservative areas.
- **Arguments and confrontation:** Keep disagreements calm and respectful. If a situation feels tense, step away rather than escalating it.
- **Alcohol:** Alcohol is subject to local laws and restrictions and is not widely available in ordinary public settings. Do not drink in public or assume that a restaurant serves alcohol; check local rules and venue policies.
- **Smoking:** Follow no-smoking signs and local restrictions, and avoid smoking around children, entrances, or crowded spaces.

## Visiting Markets and Rural Communities

Markets, villages, and smaller towns can offer memorable encounters, but they are also places where visitors should be thoughtful guests.

- Ask before entering private property, photographing homes, or taking pictures of people at work.
- Bargaining is common in some markets, but not everywhere. Keep it friendly, and accept a price if you are comfortable with it rather than turning negotiation into a confrontation.
- Dress modestly when visiting rural communities or religious areas, and follow local guidance.
- Do not assume that customs in one community represent all of Bangladesh. The country includes different religious communities, ethnic groups, and regional traditions.

## Practical Ways to Show Respect

You do not need to know every custom before arriving. These simple habits go a long way:

1. Observe the setting and follow posted instructions.
2. Ask before photographing people or entering sacred and private spaces.
3. Dress modestly when unsure, especially at religious sites.
4. Be patient, speak politely, and avoid making jokes about religion or local customs.
5. Respect a person's choice not to shake hands, be photographed, or accept an invitation.

Cultural etiquette is about consideration, not perfection. If you make an unintentional mistake, a brief apology and a willingness to follow local guidance are usually the best response.
