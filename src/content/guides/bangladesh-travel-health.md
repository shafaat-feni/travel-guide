A stomach upset, a bad mosquito bite, or an unexpected injury can disrupt a trip quickly. A little preparation before flying to Bangladesh makes it easier to enjoy the country and deal with problems if they arise. Health risks vary with the season, length of stay, activities, and places you plan to visit, so use this guide alongside advice from a qualified travel-health professional.

**Before you travel:** Arrange a travel-health appointment ideally 4–6 weeks before departure. Some vaccines need more than one dose, and malaria prevention depends on your itinerary. Check the latest advice from the <a href="https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/bangladesh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CDC's Bangladesh travel-health page</a> and your own country's health authority.

## Arrange Travel Medical Insurance Before Departure

Medical care can be straightforward for minor problems in major cities, but a serious illness or injury away from a well-equipped hospital can become complicated and expensive. Check that your policy covers emergency treatment and, if medically necessary, evacuation or repatriation.

## Vaccinations and Pre-Trip Health Advice

There is no single vaccine list that suits every visitor. Your clinician can recommend vaccines based on your medical history, previous immunisations, trip length, and planned activities.

- **Routine vaccinations:** Make sure routine vaccines are up to date. This commonly includes measles-mumps-rubella (MMR), tetanus-diphtheria-pertussis, polio, and seasonal influenza or COVID-19 vaccination where recommended for you.
- **Hepatitis A:** Commonly recommended for unvaccinated travellers because infection can spread through contaminated food or water.
- **Typhoid:** Often recommended, especially for visitors staying with friends or relatives, travelling to smaller towns or rural areas, or eating in settings where food hygiene may be uncertain.
- **Hepatitis B:** May be recommended depending on your vaccination history, length of stay, and likelihood of potential exposure, including medical care or activities that could involve blood contact.
- **Rabies:** Discuss this vaccine if you will spend substantial time outdoors, visit remote areas, work with animals, or may have difficulty reaching prompt medical care. Any bite or scratch still needs urgent medical assessment, even if you were vaccinated before travelling.
- **Japanese encephalitis:** May be considered for some longer stays or itineraries involving rural areas, particularly where there is significant outdoor exposure. Ask a travel-health clinician whether it applies to your trip.
- **Yellow fever:** Bangladesh is not generally considered a yellow-fever-risk destination, but an entry certificate may be required if you arrive from a country with yellow fever transmission or have a qualifying transit history. Check current entry requirements for your exact route.

Do not start or stop prescription medicines based only on a general travel guide. If you have a chronic condition, take enough regular medication for the trip, keep it in its original packaging where practical, and carry a copy of your prescription.

## Food and Water Safety

You do not need to avoid local food to eat more safely in Bangladesh. The main aim is to choose food that has been handled well and served at a safe temperature.

### Drinking water

- Choose factory-sealed bottled water from a reliable shop, and check that the seal is intact.
- If safe bottled water is unavailable, use water treated by a reliable method recommended by a health professional or public-health authority. Do not assume that clear water is safe to drink.
- Use safe water for brushing your teeth if you are unsure about the local supply.
- Avoid ice unless you know it was made from safe water.
- Carry water when travelling, particularly in hot weather or on long road journeys.

### Eating safely

- Choose food that is freshly cooked and served hot.
- Busy stalls can have quick turnover, but popularity alone does not guarantee good hygiene. Watch whether food is cooked thoroughly and handled with clean utensils.
- Be cautious with raw salads, unpeeled fruit, unpasteurised dairy products, and food left uncovered or sitting lukewarm for long periods.
- Peel fruit yourself when possible and wash your hands before eating.
- Use hand sanitiser when soap and clean water are not available.

Diarrhoea can happen even when you take care. Oral rehydration salts (ORS) are useful to carry and should be mixed with safe water exactly as directed on the packet. Seek medical advice for blood in the stool, high fever, severe or persistent pain, repeated vomiting, signs of dehydration, or symptoms that are not improving. Children, older adults, pregnant travellers, and people with underlying conditions may need care sooner.

## Mosquitoes, Dengue and Malaria

Mosquito-borne illnesses are a consideration in Bangladesh. Dengue can occur throughout the year, with risk often rising during and after periods of heavy rain. Mosquitoes that spread dengue may bite during the day, so protection is not only a dawn-and-dusk concern.

### Reduce mosquito bites

- Apply an effective repellent according to its label. Products containing DEET or picaridin are commonly used; follow the product instructions, especially for children.
- Wear lightweight long sleeves and trousers when practical.
- Choose accommodation with air conditioning, intact window screens, or a properly fitted bed net where needed.
- Use a bed net when sleeping in a place without adequate protection.
- Cover containers of standing water around accommodation when you can do so safely, and avoid areas with heavy mosquito activity.

### What about malaria?

Malaria risk is not uniform across Bangladesh. Some itineraries—particularly those involving certain forested or hilly areas—may require a different level of precaution from a short stay in central Dhaka. Ask a travel-health clinician whether preventive medicine is recommended for your exact route. Do not buy or take antimalarial medication without professional guidance.

If you develop a fever during your trip or after returning home, seek medical advice promptly and tell the clinician where in Bangladesh you travelled. Dengue, malaria, and other infections can begin with similar symptoms, and the right assessment matters.

## Heat, Sun and Air Quality

Heat and humidity can be tiring, particularly if you arrive from a cooler climate or plan long days outdoors.

- Drink fluids regularly and take breaks in shade or air-conditioned places.
- Wear a hat, lightweight clothing, and sunscreen; reapply sunscreen as directed.
- Build up activity gradually and avoid pushing through dizziness, confusion, severe weakness, or signs of heat illness.
- Check local air-quality information if you have asthma or another respiratory condition, and reduce strenuous outdoor activity when pollution is high.
- In heavy rain or flooding, avoid walking or driving through floodwater, which may conceal hazards and be contaminated.

## Medical Care in Bangladesh

Dhaka and other major cities have private hospitals and clinics used by international visitors. Facilities and services vary, and care may be more limited in rural areas, on islands, and in remote parts of the country. English-speaking staff may be available at some private facilities, but this cannot be guaranteed.

Before leaving home, save the contact details for your insurer's emergency-assistance team and identify a suitable medical facility near your first accommodation. For remote trips, ask your tour operator how medical help and transport would be arranged if someone became seriously ill or injured.

When seeking care:

- Contact your insurer's assistance service as early as possible if you may need hospital treatment or evacuation. Follow the policy's instructions on approval and documentation.
- Keep your passport or a copy, insurance details, medication list, and relevant medical records accessible.
- Ask about costs and payment arrangements before non-emergency treatment where possible.
- Do not delay urgent care while trying to settle insurance paperwork.

Do not rely on a general travel page for emergency numbers or a facility's current status. Confirm local emergency contacts and hospital details shortly before departure.

## Medicines and a Small Travel Health Kit

Bring personal prescription medicines in their original labelled packaging, plus a copy of your prescription or a doctor's letter when appropriate. Check the rules for carrying controlled medicines into Bangladesh before travelling.

A compact kit may include:

- Oral rehydration salts (ORS)
- Your usual prescription medicines and any clinician-recommended travel medicines
- A digital thermometer
- Plasters and basic wound-care supplies
- Hand sanitiser and tissues
- Insect repellent and sunscreen
- Any over-the-counter medicines you normally use safely

Do not use antibiotics preventively unless a clinician specifically advises you to. Avoid taking medicines from unlabelled packaging or from a source you cannot verify.

## If You Get Sick During or After Your Trip

Get urgent medical help for difficulty breathing, fainting, confusion, severe dehydration, persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, blood in vomit or stool, or any rapidly worsening symptoms.

A fever after mosquito exposure deserves prompt assessment. If dengue is possible, seek medical advice before taking aspirin or ibuprofen, as these medicines may increase bleeding risk in dengue. A clinician can advise on appropriate fever relief and testing.

Some travel-related infections can appear after you return home. Tell your doctor that you visited Bangladesh, explain the dates and areas you travelled to, and mention any fever, animal bite, or significant insect exposure—even if the trip ended weeks or months earlier.
